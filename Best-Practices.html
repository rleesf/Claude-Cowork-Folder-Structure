Best Practices: The Top 10 Folder Systems

The ten folder systems people use, the principles behind them, naming rules, and a recommendation on which to choose.

Ten systems, in depthUpdated: 2026-06-19

The ten folder systems people actually use, with the principles behind them, the trade-offs of each, the naming rules the sources agree on, and a recommendation on which to choose. Source links are at the bottom and on the Sources page.

Four Principles Behind Every System

Every system below is a different answer to the same four ideas. Get these right and any structure works.

PrincipleWhat it means
One home per areaEvery project and every task has a single obvious folder. Top levels are projects, stages, or categories, not stray files. Removes the "where did I put it" question. By default, without the proper manual setup, you will end up with a mess over time.
Stay shallowKeep the tree to roughly 2 to 4 levels. Go wide before deep. Past 3 to 4 levels, navigation slows and files get lost.
Name for sortingGeneral to specific, no spaces or special characters, dates as YYYYMMDD, versions as v01. Files group and sort themselves.
Consistency, written downPick one convention, repeat it, and keep a short index or README that records the rules. Consistency is what makes a system survive.

The Top 10 Systems

Systems 1 to 4 are the common choices for a personal or business workspace. Systems 5 to 7 are the input-to-output workflow family. The last three are specialist or classic methods. Each card is collapsed, click to open.

1 · PARA Personal knowledge

Personal knowledge

By Tiago Forte, from Building a Second Brain (2022). Four top folders sorted by how soon you act on the contents, not by topic: Projects (active, with a deadline), Areas (ongoing responsibilities), Resources (reference topics), and Archive (inactive items).

Second-Brain/
├── 1-Projects/      # active, has a deadline
├── 2-Areas/         # ongoing responsibilities
├── 3-Resources/     # reference topics
└── 4-Archive/       # inactive items
ProsCons
+ Scales to your whole digital life, works in any tool.− The four buckets take a moment to internalize.
+ Sorting by actionability keeps live work in front.− "Area or Resource" can stall you.

Best for: anyone organizing many kinds of work and reference together.

2 · Johnny Decimal Numbered

Numbered

Up to 10 areas (0 to 9), each with up to 10 categories, giving 100 fixed numbered addresses like 11.03. An index, the JDex, lists every number. Low numbers go to what you use most.

10-19 Admin/
├── 11 Finance/
│   ├── 11.01 invoices
│   └── 11.02 receipts
└── 12 Contracts/
20-29 Projects/
└── 21 Website-Redesign/
ProsCons
+ Folders sort themselves, numbers become shorthand.− Upfront effort to assign the map.
+ An index keeps everything findable and auditable.− The 10 by 10 cap forces hard choices.

Best for: people who want a fixed sort order or share folders with a team. Pairs well with PARA.

3 · GTD (Getting Things Done) Action-based

Action-based

From David Allen's method. Folders mirror your workflow rather than topics, so the structure answers "what is the next action." A common layout: Inbox, Projects, Waiting-For, Someday-Maybe, and a deep Reference store for everything non-actionable.

GTD/
├── Inbox/          # capture here first
├── Projects/       # active multi-step work
├── Waiting-For/    # delegated, pending others
├── Someday-Maybe/  # later, not now
└── Reference/      # non-actionable, keep for lookup
ProsCons
+ Keeps attention on what is actionable now.− Reference folder can balloon without its own order.
+ Pairs naturally with a task manager.− Needs a regular review to stay accurate.

Best for: people who already run their tasks on GTD and want files to match.

4 · Project or Client-Based Project-based

Project-based

Top-level folders are the projects or clients themselves. Inside each, you repeat the same standard set of subfolders every time, so any project is instantly navigable. The most recommended setup for freelancers, agencies, and consultants.

Client-Northwind/
├── Invoices/
├── Assets/
├── Drafts/
└── Final-Delivery/
ProsCons
+ Maps directly to how project work happens.− Root grows long once you pass ~20 projects.
+ The repeated subfolders build muscle memory.− Needs a category layer or archive to stay tidy.

Best for: anyone juggling distinct projects or clients.

5 · Input, Process, Output Workflow / input-output

Workflow / input-output

The minimal workflow structure: at least three folders for input, working (or src), and output. The point is a linear, one-direction flow so you always know what is raw and what is finished.

project/
├── 01-input/     # raw, never edited
├── 02-working/   # scripts, drafts, intermediate
└── 03-output/    # finished, shareable results
ProsCons
+ You never confuse source material with results.− Built around one project, not a whole workspace.
+ Reproducible: rerun the process, regenerate output.− Less useful for pure reference or admin files.

Best for: any repeatable process where raw inputs become finished outputs.

6 · Cookiecutter Data Science Workflow / input-output

Workflow / input-output

A standardized template from DrivenData for analysis projects. It is the input-to-output idea formalized: raw data is read-only input, processed data and models are intermediate, and reports are the output. A whole community shares the same layout.

project/
├── data/        # raw (read-only), processed
├── notebooks/
├── src/
├── models/
├── reports/     # the output
└── README
ProsCons
+ Reproducible and instantly recognizable to others.− Aimed at code and data, not general documents.
+ Raw-data-is-sacred rule prevents accidental edits.− Overkill for non-technical work.

Best for: data, analysis, and modeling projects that should be reproducible.

7 · Creative / Agency Stage-Based Workflow / input-output

Workflow / input-output

Used across design and post-production. Numbered stage folders move work from brief to delivery. Assets are the inputs, deliverables are the outputs, and revisions stay separate from approved files.

project/
├── 00_admin/
├── 01_brief-research/
├── 02_assets/          # inputs
├── 03_working/
├── 04_approved/
├── 05_deliverables/    # outputs
└── 99_archive/
ProsCons
+ Matches how creative work actually progresses.− Many folders per project to maintain.
+ Approved and exported files never get mixed up.− Heavier than small projects need.

Best for: design, branding, video, and other deliverable-driven creative work.

8 · Date-Based / Chronological Time-based

Time-based

Top folders are years, then months, in YYYY and YYYY-MM form so they always sort in time order. Best where the natural question is "when," not "what."

2026/
├── 2026-01/
├── 2026-02/
└── 2026-03/ ...
ProsCons
+ Perfect for journals, receipts, recurring reports.− Useless when you forget the date of a thing.
+ Sorts itself, no thinking required.− Mixes unrelated topics in one month.

Best for: time-series material: statements, photos, meeting notes, periodic reports.

9 · Zettelkasten + Maps of Content Personal knowledge

Personal knowledge

A note-network approach. Instead of deep folders, you keep notes mostly flat and connect them with links. An index note, a Map of Content, acts as the table of contents you navigate from.

notes/                  # flat pool of linked notes
├── MOC-Marketing.md    # index note, links the rest
├── idea-001.md
└── idea-002.md
ProsCons
+ Great for ideas that connect across topics.− Needs a linking tool like Obsidian to shine.
+ Avoids the "which folder" problem entirely.− Weak for binary files like docs and images.

Best for: writers and researchers building a linked knowledge base.

10 · ACCESS / Frequency-Based Action-based

Action-based

Organizes by how often you reach for something. The files you touch daily sit closest and most visible, rarely used material gets pushed down or archived. The aim is the fewest clicks to your most-used items.

Files/
├── Active/      # daily, front and center
├── Occasional/  # now and then
└── Archive/     # rarely used, out of the way
ProsCons
+ Optimizes for retrieval speed of common files.− "Frequent" shifts over time, needs upkeep.
+ Simple to grasp and apply.− Less precise than a named category system.

Best for: people who want speed to a small set of files they use constantly.

Recommendations: Which System to Choose

No single system suits everyone. The right choice depends on how much work you hold, how you naturally think, and whether your work runs as a repeatable pipeline. In practice, most people are served best not by one system but by a small, layered combination.

If you are unsure, this layered blueprint covers most needs by borrowing the strongest idea from several systems at once: project folders for structure, a single archive to keep the active view short, light numbers for a predictable order, and input, working, and output folders inside any project that runs as a pipeline.

Workspace/
├── 1-Projects/              # one folder per active project
│   └── Example-Project/
│       ├── input/           # raw source material
│       ├── working/         # drafts and scratch
│       └── output/          # finished deliverables
├── 2-Areas/                 # ongoing responsibilities
├── 3-Resources/             # reference by topic
└── 9-Archive/               # inactive, keeps the active view short
If you haveUse
A handful of distinct projects or clientsProject-based (4). One folder per project mirrors how the work is already divided.
A growing mix of active work and reference materialPARA (1). Sorting by how soon you will act keeps live work in front.
You want a fixed, predictable order, or share folders with a teamJohnny Decimal (2). Numbered addresses sort themselves and become a shared shorthand.
Your work turns raw inputs into finished outputs, again and againInput, process, output (5), or Cookiecutter (6) for data.
Deliverable-driven creative productionCreative stage-based (7). Numbered stages carry work from brief to delivery.
Mostly time-stamped material such as notes or receiptsDate-based (8). Folders sort themselves by time.
A connected web of notes and ideasZettelkasten with a Map of Content (9). The connections matter more than the folders.
