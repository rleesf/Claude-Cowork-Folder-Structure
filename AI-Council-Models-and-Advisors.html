The AI Council Playbook

Summary

Start here

An AI council is a panel of distinct advisor minds you convene around a single decision. Each seat is built to think differently, one to challenge, one to expand, one to count the cost, one to act, and a chair to weigh it all and rule.

The instinct is old. Marvin Minsky argued in 1986 that intelligence itself emerges from many simple minds working together. The AI version arrived fast: a widely read MIT Sloan piece on the personal board of advisors in mid 2025, Andrej Karpathy's open-source LLM Council in late 2025, and Perplexity's Model Council in early 2026. They share one motivation. A lone AI tends to agree with you, and a council is the cure. the Foundations section below tells the fuller story.

You do not have to design the table yourself. Practitioners and researchers have already established the councils worth knowing, from a five-advisor decision panel to a coding council modeled on a real software team. The cards below are the catalog. Choose the one that fits your decision, adjust a seat or two, and convene it.

The principle behind every council here
Telling an AI it is a "world-class expert" does not make its facts more accurate, a 2025 University of Pennsylvania study confirmed as much. A council earns its keep by widening perspective and forcing genuine disagreement on judgment calls. Convene one to pressure-test a decision, and verify the underlying facts on their own.

▸I
Foundations: What It Is, How It Began, and Why It Works
The Plain-English Background, for Newcomers

If the idea of an AI council is new to you, start here. This section explains what a council is in everyday terms, where it came from, why it produces better answers than asking one AI once, and the handful of words you will meet along the way.

▸What an AI Council Is

An AI council is a way of writing your request, not a paid feature or a button. You instruct the AI to answer from several named advisor viewpoints, let them disagree, then have a chair combine everything into one verdict. The same setup runs on a free account or across several paid models.

Picture a small boardroom. You bring a decision. A handful of advisors, each chosen to think differently, weigh in, and a chair sums up the meeting with a clear call. An AI council is exactly this, run by AI. One model can play every seat, or different models can each take a chair.

▸How the Idea Developed
  • 1986, Society of Mind. Marvin Minsky argued that intelligence emerges from many simple agents interacting. The intellectual root of every multi-agent system.
  • 2018, debate as a method. Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, and Dario Amodei at OpenAI proposed "AI Safety via Debate": let two AIs argue opposing sides while a judge decides, on the theory that judging an argument is easier than making one.
  • May 2023, multi-agent debate proven. A team at MIT (Du, Torralba, Tenenbaum, and Mordatch) showed that having several model instances debate and critique each other measurably improves factual accuracy and reasoning over a single answer.
  • Mid-2023, role-based agent teams. Frameworks like ChatDev, MetaGPT, and Microsoft's AutoGen cast LLMs as a staffed team, a product manager, an engineer, a reviewer, that collaborates through defined roles.
  • 2024, Mixture-of-Agents. Wang and colleagues layered several open models so each refines the others' answers, and the ensemble beat a single top model on standard benchmarks.
  • July 2025, the personal board. MIT Sloan Management Review published an executive's account of building a personal board of directors from AI personas modeled on figures like Steve Jobs and Indra Nooyi. One of its most-read pieces of the year.
  • November 2025, the LLM Council. Andrej Karpathy released a free, open-source app that sends a question to several models, has them rank each other anonymously, then a chair model writes the answer. Tens of thousands of developers starred it.
  • February 2026, Model Council goes mainstream. Perplexity launched Model Council, running three frontier models at once and reconciling them for everyday users.
  • 2026, the skill wave. Practitioners such as Ole Lehmann and Allie K. Miller packaged single-model, five-to-eight-advisor councils into reusable skills, and the format spread across YouTube and X.
▸Why a Council Works

AI assistants are trained to be helpful and agreeable, which makes them lean toward telling you what you want to hear. Researchers call this sycophancy.

The evidence
A Stanford-led study in the journal Science (2026) tested 11 leading models and found they affirmed a user's intended action roughly 50% more often than other people did, even when the action showed poor judgment. People who received the agreeable advice grew more certain they were right and rated the flattering model as better, so the bias is easy to miss.

A council counters this in three ways. An assigned critic looks only for what fails, so the criticism a single agreeable answer hides gets said out loud. Advisors answer independently before comparing notes, which preserves real differences instead of collapsing into easy agreement. And an anonymized review step lets the AI judge the answers honestly rather than defend its own.

▸The Honest Evidence

A council is a tool, not magic. The balanced picture:

  • Single AIs flatter you. Well documented (above), and the core reason to seek structured disagreement.
  • Debate helps, but not always. Multi-agent debate can raise accuracy, yet several 2025 studies found it often fails to beat a cheaper "sample many times and vote" baseline.
  • A bad council goes wrong. A 2026 study in Scientific Reports (a Nature journal) showed one confident, persuasive but mistaken advisor can drop a group's accuracy by 10 to 40 percent, and adding more advisors does not reliably fix it.
  • Personas do not improve facts. The University of Pennsylvania (2025) found expert-persona labels do not make answers more factually accurate.
The takeaway
Use a council to widen perspective and force dissent on judgment calls. Keep a human as the decider, and verify the underlying facts on their own.

Ways to Group the Councils
Five Plain Questions That Describe Almost Any Council

There are dozens of council types, but they sort along a few simple axes. Answer these five questions and you have described nearly any council, including all the ones above.

The questionWhat it settlesThe options
1. Who is on it?CompositionOne AI playing many roles, or several different AI models, each a seat.
2. How do they talk?InteractionAnswer separately and compare (parallel), argue it out (debate), vote (consensus), or a lead assigns the work (orchestrator).
3. What is it for?PurposeDeciding, researching, brainstorming, grading, building, or stress-testing.
4. Do they agree or fight?RelationshipA friendly team, deliberate opponents, or a mix of both.
5. What is it called, and how big?Theme & sizeBoardroom, roundtable, war room, usually 3 to 8 seats.
Which of these actually matter
The first two questions carry the weight, and they are the parts research has formally mapped. "How do they talk" and "do they agree or fight" come straight from multi-agent studies. Purpose, domain, and size are practical groupings. The name is flavor, not function, so a war room is a boardroom in a hurry, not a different machine.

▸III
Best Practices From the Field
The Pattern Across Researchers, Practitioners, and Communities
  • The origin: Andrej Karpathy's open-source LLM Council, where models answer, rank each other blind, and a chair synthesizes.
  • The two camps: true multi-model councils (different vendors, real diversity, pay per token) and single-model councils (one model playing five to eight advisors, cheaper and faster), the latter popularized by Ole Lehmann and Allie K. Miller.
  • The motivation: stopping a single AI from agreeing with you. The recurring moves are anonymized peer review and a chair.
  • The agreed scope: convene a council for high-stakes judgment calls, not for facts or summaries, and read the disagreements rather than the tidy summary.
The reputable caveat
The University of Pennsylvania's Wharton AI Lab found that expert-persona labels do not improve factual accuracy. A council surfaces blind spots and forces dissent, but it does not make the model smarter about facts, so verify those on their own.

▸Pre-Mortem, Disney, and Red / Blue / Purple Prompts
Pre-mortem
It is 12 months from now and this decision was a disaster: [DECISION].
Act as a blunt post-failure investigator. List exactly what went wrong, ranked from
most to least likely, with the early warning signs I ignored. Do not reassure me.
Then give the top 3 mitigations to put in place now.
Disney Method
Act as the Disney Method in three rounds for: [CHALLENGE].
Round 1, Dreamer: the most ambitious ideas, no feasibility filter.
Round 2, Realist: turn the best idea into a concrete plan (steps, resources, timeline).
Round 3, Critic: attack the plan only, not the vision; list flaws, risks, and gaps.
Red / Blue / Purple team
Run a Red/Blue/Purple exercise on my plan: [PLAN].
Blue: state the plan and its strongest defense.
Red: act as a hostile competitor and attack it; list realistic ways it loses.
Purple: reconcile both, rank Red's attacks by likelihood, and give the changes that
defend against the credible ones. Output a hardened plan.

▸V
The Councils, In Depth
Each Council in Full, With a Ready-to-Paste Prompt

Each council is a different answer to one question: which kinds of disagreement does this decision most need? Each one below is a full write-up with its seats and a ready-to-paste prompt. Pick the one that fits and convene it.

▸How to Choose and Tune a Council
  • Match the disagreement to the decision. A launch needs a Skeptic and a Pre-Mortem. A creative call needs a Visionary and an Outsider. A money call needs a Bear and a CFO.
  • Size to the stakes. Three seats for speed, five as the default, seven or eight when being wrong is expensive.
  • Swap, don't rebuild. Take any council below and replace a seat or two from the advisor roster in The Advisors below.
  • Always seat a chair. One advisor must read the room and make the call, or you are left with opinions and no decision.
  • Let them answer alone first. Independence preserves real differences, and sharing too early collapses the room into agreement.
▸The LLM Council

The original, from Andrej Karpathy. Instead of trusting one model, you put a question to several rival models at once. They answer independently, then rank each other's answers with the authors hidden so none can play favorites, and a designated Chairman model writes the final response. You see where the best models agree, and more usefully, where they do not.

  • The Members (Claude, GPT, Gemini, Grok): each gives an independent first opinion to the same prompt.
  • The Anonymous Reviewers: the same models, re-tasked to rank every answer blind to who wrote it.
  • The Chairman: one model compiles the ranked answers into a single verdict.
When to convene it
Hard, open questions where cross-model consensus is worth the extra cost, and where seeing the disagreement is itself the insight. Source: Karpathy's open-source repository, around 21,000 stars.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Run an LLM Council on my question, in one chat. Question: [QUESTION].
1. Answer it three times, independently, as three different expert minds (A, B, C).
   Do not let them influence each other.
2. Now review them blind: rank answers A, B, and C on accuracy and insight, as if you
   do not know which is which, and say why.
3. As Chairman, compile the highest-ranked points into one clear verdict, and note
   exactly where the three disagreed.
▸The Technical & Coding Council

A software team rendered as advisors, grounded in the research frameworks MetaGPT and ChatDev. Work moves down an assembly line, and crucially the reviewer is kept independent of the author, so it judges the code rather than defending the plan that produced it.

  • The Product Manager: turns the request into clear requirements and acceptance criteria.
  • The Architect: sets the structure, interfaces, and trade-offs.
  • The Engineer: writes the implementation.
  • The Reviewer: reviews the diff for correctness, seeing only the change, not the backstory.
  • The Security Auditor: hunts injection, secrets, and access flaws.
  • The QA Tester: writes and runs the tests that prove it works.
When to convene it
Designing, building, or reviewing real software, especially complex changes. The reviewer, security, and QA seats are the ones that catch what a single pass misses. Sources: MetaGPT and ChatDev (peer-published).
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Run a Technical Council on this work. Task or code: [PASTE THE SPEC OR DIFF].
Answer as six roles, in order, each in its own voice:
1. Product Manager: restate the requirements and acceptance criteria; flag ambiguity.
2. Architect: the structure, interfaces, and the key trade-offs.
3. Engineer: the cleanest implementation approach.
4. Reviewer: review only the change for correctness bugs; do not defend the plan.
5. Security Auditor: injection, secrets, access, and dependency risk, rated by severity.
6. QA Tester: the tests, including edge and failure cases, that would prove it works.
Then list the must-fix issues and the single first step.
▸The 5-Advisor Council

The 2026 default for any judgment call. Five thinking-style advisors answer independently, review each other blind, then a Chairman decides. Its whole job is to stop a single AI from quietly agreeing with you.

  • The Contrarian: names what will fail and the worst plausible outcome.
  • The First-Principles Thinker: asks whether you are solving the right problem.
  • The Expansionist: finds the upside being undersold.
  • The Outsider: brings the obvious question insiders stopped asking.
  • The Executor: names the first concrete step this week.
When to convene it
Almost any decision worth a second opinion. Source: Ole Lehmann, adapting Karpathy's LLM Council.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Be my 5-Advisor Council. Decision: [DECISION].
Five advisors answer independently, then a Chairman decides. They may disagree.
1. The Contrarian: what fails, and the worst plausible outcome.
2. The First-Principles Thinker: am I solving the right problem?
3. The Expansionist: the upside I am underselling.
4. The Outsider: the obvious question insiders skip.
5. The Executor: the first concrete step this week.
Chairman: the decision, the biggest risk, and the first step. No hedging.
▸The Boardroom

The everyday workhorse. Four advisors debate your call and hand back a clear board summary that names where they agreed, where they clashed, and the one question still open.

  • The Pragmatic Operator: what actually gets done, and how.
  • The Long-term Strategist: where this leads in three years.
  • The Blunt Skeptic: what breaks, stated plainly.
  • The Wise Mentor: the perspective you are missing.
When to convene it
Everyday business and personal decisions. Source: PCWorld board-of-advisors prompt.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Act as my Boardroom of four advisors. Decision: [DECISION].
1. The Pragmatic Operator: what actually gets done, and how.
2. The Long-term Strategist: where this leads in three years.
3. The Blunt Skeptic: what breaks, stated plainly.
4. The Wise Mentor: the perspective I am missing.
Then give a board summary: where they agree, where they clash, and the one question
I still need to answer.
▸The Business Advisory Board

A board of named business figures you respect, each arguing in their real thinking style. It is the personal board of advisors made concrete: pick mentors whose strengths cover the decision, and let them clash before a chair gives the call. Swap the names for figures from your own industry.

  • The Growth Operator (for example Alex Hormozi): the offer, the funnel, and what actually moves revenue.
  • The Capital Allocator (for example Warren Buffett): long-term value and what to avoid.
  • The Marketer (for example Seth Godin): the positioning and who this is really for.
  • The Startup Builder (for example Paul Graham): talk to users, what to build next, staying alive.
  • The Customer Champion (for example Jeff Bezos): work backward from the customer.
  • The Chair: weighs the board and gives the recommendation.
When to convene it
Business and growth decisions you want pressure-tested by mentors you admire. Treat each voice as a thinking style, not a quotation, and verify any specific claim. Source: the personal board of advisors pattern (MIT Sloan, 2025, and PCWorld).
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Convene a Business Advisory Board on this decision. Decision: [BUSINESS DECISION].
Seat these advisors, each arguing in their real thinking style, a style and not quotes:
1. The Growth Operator (for example Alex Hormozi): the offer, the funnel, and what moves revenue.
2. The Capital Allocator (for example Warren Buffett): the long-term value and what to avoid.
3. The Marketer (for example Seth Godin): the positioning and who this is really for.
4. The Startup Builder (for example Paul Graham): talk to users, what to build next, staying alive.
5. The Customer Champion (for example Jeff Bezos): work backward from the customer.
Then a Chair: where they agree, where they clash, and the recommended move. Verify any specific claim yourself.
▸The Voting Boardroom

The most-copied advanced format. Each advisor is a named figure with a short personality. In round one they answer independently and cast a vote, and in round two they read every other answer and may change it.

  • Named figures: each a distinct worldview you choose for the decision.
  • The vote: YES, NO, or CONDITIONAL, tracked across two rounds.
  • The synthesis: the tally, the real tensions, and the recommendation.
When to convene it
High-stakes calls you want genuinely argued out. Source: Allie K. Miller's AI boardroom of named advisors debating over two rounds, and the explicit YES/NO/CONDITIONAL vote is an added convention.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Run a Voting Boardroom on my decision. Decision: [DECISION].
Seat five named advisors, each a distinct worldview (suggest fitting figures, or I will name them).
Round 1: each answers independently and casts a vote, YES, NO, or CONDITIONAL, with one reason.
Round 2: each reads the others and may change their vote, saying why.
Finish with the vote tally, the real tensions, and your recommendation.
▸The Investment Committee

A bull and a bear argue the thesis to its limits while a risk officer and a chair render the call. Modeled on how real investment committees pressure-test a deal.

  • Fundamentals & Sentiment: the case from the numbers and the narrative.
  • The Bull: the strongest reasons to commit.
  • The Bear: the strongest reasons to pass, run against the Bull.
  • The Risk Officer: exposure, drawdown, and position sizing.
  • The Chair: the final memo with a confidence rating, not a coin flip.
When to convene it
Go/no-go calls and capital allocation. Source: the bull-vs-bear structure of TradingAgents.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Convene an Investment Committee. Opportunity: [DEAL OR BET].
1. Fundamentals & Sentiment: the case from the numbers and the narrative.
2. The Bull: the strongest reasons to commit.
3. The Bear: the strongest reasons to pass, argued directly against the Bull.
4. The Risk Officer: exposure, drawdown, and position sizing.
5. The Chair: a final memo with a clear recommendation and a confidence level, not a coin flip.
▸The War Room

For a crisis, you do not want a fixed panel. A Scenario Analyst sizes up the threat and recruits the specialists it needs, who then work the problem fast under a single Chairman.

  • The Scenario Analyst: frames the threat and picks the team.
  • On-demand specialists: only the experts this crisis actually needs.
  • The Chairman: holds the timeline and makes the call under pressure.
When to convene it
Time-pressured crises, incidents, and competitive threats. Source: Komodor's "war room of AI agents" pattern, an incident commander that recruits specialist agents.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Run a War Room on this crisis. Situation: [CRISIS].
1. Scenario Analyst: frame the threat and name exactly which specialists we need.
2. Bring in those specialists; each gives their fastest read and recommended action.
3. Chairman: resolve conflicts, hold the timeline, and give the decision and the first
   three moves to make now.
Prioritize speed and the most likely failure path.
▸The Round Table

Named experts you admire sit as equals and question you Socratically rather than hand you tidy answers. You leave with sharper thinking, not a verdict.

  • Three to five named experts: chosen for genuinely different lenses.
  • Equal voice: no chair overrides, and the tension is the point.
  • Socratic stance: they probe your assumptions before advising.
When to convene it
When you want to be challenged by voices you respect. Source: The Prompt Warrior's experts roundtable.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Host a Round Table for me. Topic: [TOPIC].
Seat three to five named experts I respect, as equals with no chair (suggest fitting ones, or I will name them).
They question me Socratically about my assumptions and evidence before offering any view,
and they may disagree with each other.
End with the sharpest open questions I should sit with, not a tidy verdict.
▸The Debate Council

Two advisors take opposite sides of the same question and argue it out in rounds, then a neutral judge weighs the exchange and rules. Forcing the strongest case both for and against surfaces the real trade-off before you commit.

  • The Advocate: builds the strongest possible case for the proposal.
  • The Challenger: builds the strongest possible case against, answering the Advocate directly.
  • The Rebuttal: each side gets one reply to the other's best point.
  • The Judge: weighs the exchange, rules, and gives a confidence level.
When to convene it
Binary or either/or decisions where you want the strongest case on both sides before choosing. Sources: "AI Safety via Debate" (Irving, Christiano, Amodei, 2018) and multi-agent debate research (Du et al., 2023, and Khan et al., ICML 2024).
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Run a Debate on this decision. Question: [YES/NO OR EITHER/OR DECISION].
1. The Advocate: make the strongest possible case FOR.
2. The Challenger: make the strongest possible case AGAINST, answering the Advocate directly.
3. Rebuttal: each side gets one reply to the other's single best point.
4. The Judge: decide which side argued better and why, then give the ruling and a confidence level.
▸Red Team / Blue Team

A blue team defends a plan as sound while a red team attacks it like an adversary trying to make it fail. A referee then lists the real vulnerabilities and what must be fixed before you proceed. The structure is borrowed straight from security practice.

  • The Blue Team: defends the plan and explains why it holds.
  • The Red Team: attacks it, finding the failure modes and the weak assumptions.
  • The Referee: lists the confirmed weaknesses by severity and the fixes required.
When to convene it
Stress-testing a plan, a launch, or a security and risk decision before committing. Source: standard LLM red-teaming practice, for example the Promptfoo and DeepTeam guides.
Paste this prompt into a fresh Claude chat
Run a Red Team versus Blue Team on this plan. Plan: [PLAN].
1. Blue Team: defend the plan. Explain why it works and where it is strong.
2. Red Team: attack it like an adversary. Find the failure modes, the weak assumptions, and how it breaks.
3. Referee: list the confirmed vulnerabilities by severity, and the fixes required before going ahead.

The Advisors
The Full Roster, With the Essential Three In Depth

Every council is built from these advisors. The three below appear on nearly every table, so they are worth knowing in full. The rest follow as a roster you can draw from.

The most powerful move: cast a real person for any seat
Any seat here can be filled by a named figure instead of a generic role, and it is often the single biggest upgrade to a council. Pick someone real and well known from your own field whose thinking style fits the seat, for example a famous investor for the money seat, a respected founder for the growth seat, or a sharp critic for the Contrarian. Name them in the prompt, ask the model to reason the way they actually think, and treat the voice as a thinking style, not a quotation. Verify any specific fact or number on its own, since the model only approximates the person. The roster below has worked examples.
1

The Contrarian

The seat that earns the council its value. The Contrarian looks only for what fails, with no instinct to balance the view. It names what breaks first, the worst plausible outcome, and the assumption you are quietly relying on. Seat it on every decision, because it is the direct cure for an AI that wants to agree with you.

The question it owns: what is the strongest case that this is wrong? Seat it when: always, and especially when the room is agreeing too easily.

2

The Visionary

The counterweight to the Contrarian. The Visionary finds the upside everyone is underselling and the larger version of the move. It asks what this opens up if it works, and refuses to let caution shrink the ambition before the idea has been heard. Without it, a council talks itself out of every bold call.

The question it owns: what is the bigger prize here, and the asymmetric upside? Seat it when: the decision is about growth, creation, or a bet worth making large.

3

The Operator

The seat that turns debate into motion. The Operator ignores theory and names the first concrete step: the email to send, the call to make, the thing to ship this week. It keeps the council honest about whether the decision can actually be executed, and by whom.

The question it owns: what do I do on Monday morning, and who owns it? Seat it when: the risk is endless deliberation with no action.

  • The Strategist: thinks in markets, moats, and timing, three years out.
  • The First-Principles Thinker: strips the problem to fundamentals and asks if it is the right one.
  • The Outsider: brings the obvious question that insiders stopped asking.
  • The CFO: tests the unit economics, the cash, and the payback.
  • The Customer Advocate: speaks only for the buyer and their thirty-second problem.
  • The Ethicist: names what you should refuse to do for money.
  • The Pre-Mortem Analyst: assumes the decision failed and works backward to why.
  • The Domain Expert: brings what the craft itself demands here.
  • The Data Analyst: holds the council to base rates and real evidence.
  • The Systems Thinker: traces the second-order effects and feedback loops.
  • The Negotiator: argues the counterparty's view and the leverage in the room.
  • The Futurist: asks where the trend carries this in five years.
  • The Simplifier: cuts the decision down to the one thing that matters.
  • The Chairman: reads every voice and delivers the call, the risk, and the first step.

A Model for Each Seat
For Multi-Model Councils, How to Spread Seats Across AI Families

This section only matters if you are running a multi-model council, where different AI vendors fill different chairs. If a single model is playing every advisor, you can skip it. The reason to use more than one vendor is diversity: models from the same lab share blind spots, so a council staffed entirely from one lab tends to agree with itself.

How to use this table
Think of it as a seating chart, not a shopping list. First, decide whether you want one model or several. If several, give each seat below to a different lab, so no single training lineage dominates. You do not need every seat, three or four different families is plenty for most decisions. Then use each lab's current flagship, and confirm the exact version and a recent benchmark before you rely on it.
Names change, so pick the versions yourself

▸IX
How to Build It in Claude
From a One-Off Prompt to a Permanent Council

There are many easy ways to build a Council in Claude, and you can start off as simple as a prompt. Since this playbook and your councils both live in Claude, the methods below are all Claude-native, ordered from the quickest start to the most permanent. The prompts themselves live with each council above, so they are not repeated here.

  1. As Simple as a PromptThe fastest start, and it works on any plan. Open a fresh chat in Claude, on the web, the desktop app, or mobile, paste the ready-to-paste prompt from any council above, fill in your decision, and send. Nothing to install and nothing to set up. Best for a one-off question you want a sharper answer to. 
    Be my 5-Advisor Council on this decision: [DECISION].
Five advisors answer independently, then a Chair gives the call, the biggest risk, and the first step.
  2. Claude Desktop ProjectThe simplest way to make a council permanent, with no code. In Claude, create a Project, paste a council prompt into the Project's custom instructions, and drop any reference files into the Project's knowledge. Every new chat you open inside that Project then runs as that council automatically, with nothing to paste. Keep one Project per council, name them clearly, and you can share a Project with your team so everyone uses the same council.
  3. Claude SkillsReusable everywhere, loaded on demand. A Claude Skill is a small instruction file (a SKILL.md) that Claude pulls in only when it is relevant. Package a council as a skill and it becomes available across all of your projects and chats, in Claude Code and in Cowork, without being tied to a single Project. Several open council skills already exist, so you can install one rather than write your own.
  4. Claude Agent SDKFor a council that runs on its own. The Claude Agent SDK and subagents let a developer wire each seat as its own agent that runs in parallel, with a synthesizer agent that returns the final verdict. This is the route to automate a council inside a product, a script, or a scheduled job rather than a chat. It is the most powerful option and the only one that needs code.
  5. Bundle it as a PluginFor shipping a whole set. A plugin packages several skills, commands, and tools into one installable unit, shared through a marketplace so a team installs everything with a single command. Reach for it when you want to distribute a set of councils, or pair a council with the tools it needs, like web search or your own data. For a single council, a skill is enough. A plugin is for shipping a set or sharing widely.
One rule, whichever method you choose
Keep a human as the decider. A council widens perspective and forces disagreement, but it does not make the model more accurate on facts, so verify those yourself.
